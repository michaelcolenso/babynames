# The Glaciers — Names That Took a Generation to Rise and Fell Just as Slowly

Some names arrive like weather. Most arrive like climate.

We ran every American name since 1880 through the same detector, looking for the opposite of a flash flood. The rules: peak at 5,000 or more births in a year. A climb of at least 25 years to get there. A decline of at least 25 years coming down. No sudden arrivals, no quick deaths.

84 names qualified. We call them glaciers.

## Robert is the biggest mountain we have

Robert peaked in 1947 at 91655 boys born in a single year — and it took 35 years of steady climbing to get there. The fall took even longer. Robert was still a top-tier name six decades after its peak.

Mary tells the same story on the girls' side: 39 years up to a 73984-birth peak in 1921, then more than seventy years of slow retreat. John's decline stretched 72 years past his own peak — the longest sunset in the record.

The average glacier here rose for 39 years and fell for 47 more. A child named at the average glacier's peak would be a grandparent before the name finished falling.

## Glaciers are not old news

Only 52 percent of the glaciers are girls' names — the slow-build mega-name is mostly a boys' phenomenon. And the list is not just a museum of 1940s favorites.

Christopher is the modern outlier: a glacier that formed after television. It climbed from 60021-peak territory over roughly three decades starting in the early 1950s, crested in 1984, and only finished its decline around 2020. Barbara shows the classic mid-century shape: a 48800-birth peak in 1947 and a completed fall by the 1970s.

Sarah proves a glacier can still be warm at the edges — 28483 births at her 1982 peak, and the decline is still trailing off today.

## Why it matters

A flash flood name — Kunta, Moesha, Jkwon — is a timestamp. You can read the exact year of the TV premiere off the chart.

A glacier is a worldview. It takes two or three generations of parents, each one nudging the same name a little further up or down, to build one. Nobody chose Robert; everybody chose Robert. These are the names that shaped what America sounded like, one percent at a time — and they are also the reason your coworker's father is one of five Daves in the office.

Every glacier here has its own vital record — living population, median age, year-by-year counts — on its name page. Start with [Robert](/name/Robert/) or [Mary](/name/Mary/), or see the flash floods that did the opposite in a tenth of the time.
