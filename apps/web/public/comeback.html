Comeback Kids

Names that were dominant before 1975 — sometimes given to tens of thousands of babies a year — fell to near-zero, and are now climbing back. Emma is the prototype: practically extinct in the 1970s, #1 by 2008.

Why baby names come back

Naming culture runs in roughly century-long cycles. A name needs about three generations out of daily use before it stops sounding like someone's grandmother and starts sounding like a choice — which is why Eleanor and Theodore read as fresh today while mid-century staples like Gary and Linda still read as dated. The SSA record makes the pattern visible: a dominant name craters within a decade or two of falling from fashion, sits near zero for decades, then climbs again as vintage taste returns.

Not every dormant name revives. The ones that do tend to share traits — they were never tied to a single infamous bearer, they shorten or soften well, and they fit current sound preferences. That's why Emma came all the way back to #1 while dozens of her 1880s peers stayed retired. Browse the full lists in extinct, endangered, and rising names to see both fates.
