Two Americas, One Baby Name Book

Mateo is now one of the most common boys' names in America. Thiago has grown 130-fold in 20 years. At the same time, Maverick, Waylon, and Weston have each quintupled. The 2025 SSA data captures two distinct cultural movements — and both are winning simultaneously.

May 2026 · Based on SSA name data 1880–2025 · 117,820 name/sex pairs

Mateo: The New Classic

In 2005, Mateo was a niche choice — 1,089 babies, solidly present but nowhere near the top. By 2025, 11,045 babies were named Mateo. That puts him roughly level with Joseph and ahead of Nathan. He is no longer a rising name. He is simply a common one.

The trajectory is clean and uninterrupted — no spike, no crash, just a steady 20-year climb driven by the growth of the Latino population and the broadening of what "classic American name" means.

Santiago and Thiago: The Surge Continues

Santiago has been climbing for 20 years, but the pace has accelerated sharply: 3,000 babies in 2010, 5,000 in 2019, and 7,554 in 2025. He is now in the top 30 boys' names nationally — a position unimaginable even ten years ago.

But the most striking number belongs to Thiago. In 2005: 44 babies. In 2025: 5,835 babies. A 130-fold increase in a single generation. The Brazilian-Portuguese variant of Diego has become one of the fastest-growing names in the dataset, with no sign of slowing.

Santiago · Thiago — boys named per year · 2005–2025

Thiago's curve has a distinctive shape — gradual until around 2013, then sharply accelerating. That inflection point lines up with increased South American immigration and the rising visibility of Brazilian culture globally. Once it crossed a critical mass of familiarity, it snowballed.

Luna, Valentina, Aurora

On the girls' side, the same story is playing out. Luna peaked in 2022 at 8,977 and has settled to a very solid 6,076 in 2025 — still one of the most common girls' names in America. Valentina has been on a steady upward march and hit 5,354 in 2025, her highest ever. Aurora just keeps climbing: 7,065 in 2025 and still rising.

Luna · Valentina · Aurora — girls named per year · 2005–2025

These three names share a quality beyond their Latin roots: they feel both ancient and modern. Luna and Aurora are mythology. Valentina is history and romance. None of them feel like a trend — they feel like names that were always going to be important and are only now getting their due.

"The fastest-growing names in America aren't celebrity-driven spikes. They're names that have been climbing steadily for twenty years without stopping."

Maverick: The New Frontier

While Latin and mythological names were ascending in one part of the country, a completely different cultural movement was producing completely different names. In 2017, Top Gun: Maverick was announced. By 2018 — before the film even released — Maverick was already at 5,041 babies. He'd been climbing since 2013.

The film's release in 2022 didn't cause a spike; the name was already large enough that the movie felt like confirmation rather than cause. In 2025: 5,894 boys named Maverick. He is firmly established.

Maverick — boys named per year · 2005–2025

Maverick is the flagship of a broader category: names that signal a certain kind of American identity. Bold, frontier-adjacent, confident. Names that sound like they belong to a person who will grow up to do something interesting.

Waylon, Weston, Brooks

The names surrounding Maverick tell the same story in different registers. Waylon — the country legend's legacy — went from 365 babies in 2005 to 5,408 in 2025. Weston went from 742 to 5,482. Brooks went from 339 to 4,877.

Waylon · Weston · Brooks — boys named per year · 2005–2025

These names cluster around a coherent aesthetic: the American South and West, country music, wide spaces. Waylon carries Jennings. Weston has a topographic quality — it sounds like a place you'd want to be. Brooks is clean and outdoorsy. Together they read as a naming manifesto for a certain kind of parenthood — not vintage, not global, but rooted in a specific American grain.

Willow, Ivy, Violet

The female counterpart to this movement is the nature names. Violet has been climbing for 20 years and hit 7,546 in 2025 — her all-time record. Ivy reached 5,117. Willow is at 4,763 and still rising.

Willow · Ivy · Violet — girls named per year · 2005–2025

Nature names share a philosophy with the vintage revival names (Eleanor, Hazel, Nora) — both feel like rejections of the mid-century naming monoculture, a turn toward names that feel connected to something older and more permanent than pop culture. But where the vintage names connect to history, nature names connect to place. A Violet or a Willow sounds like someone who grew up near woods, not a suburb.

Two Trends, One Dataset

If you had to predict a culture war in baby name data, this is where you'd expect it. Two demographic movements, two aesthetic philosophies, one naming book.

But the data doesn't show a conflict. Both trends are growing. The same years that saw Mateo and Valentina rise also saw Maverick and Waylon rise. There's no zero-sum game here — the names aren't competing, they're coexisting in the same data alongside each other.

What the 2025 SSA data actually shows is a country that is simultaneously becoming more diverse and more nostalgic. More global-facing and more frontier-facing. Both impulses are producing real children with real names, right now, in the same year.

The baby name record is a mirror. In 2025, it shows two Americas — both alive, both naming themselves, both showing up in the same federal database.
