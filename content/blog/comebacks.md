# The Comebacks — Names That Nearly Died and Lived Again

Some names die and stay dead. These came back.

Every name in the record follows one of a few shapes. Flash floods spike and vanish. Glaciers rise for a generation and melt for a generation. Then there is a third shape, rarer and stranger: the name that collapses to almost nothing — and then comes back.

The rules we used: a first life that peaked at 1,000 or more births in a year. A fall to below 15% of that peak — near-death, by the record's own standard. Then a second peak of at least 500 births, arriving at least 25 years after the valley. No fakes, no near-misses.

91 names qualified. We call them comebacks.

## Emma is the greatest comeback in the record

Emma's first life peaked in 1918 at 5,324 girls a year. By 1976, she was down to 414 — a 92% collapse, deep enough that a less stubborn name would have called it a career. Then the second life arrived, and it did not tiptoe. Emma peaked in 2003 at 22,719 births — more than four times her first peak. The name came back bigger than it ever was.

That is the signature move of the comeback: the second act outperforms the first. Hollywood promises this and rarely delivers. The birth records deliver it constantly.

## The valley is always the same few years

Look at when these names bottomed out. Hazel's nadir: 1983, just 90 girls a year. Violet: 1972. The valleys cluster in the 1970s and 1980s, when the centenarian names of 1880 had finally run out of grandmothers to honor.

The pattern behind the pattern: a name has to be fully dead to be fully reborn. Names that merely dip get skipped by the revival. The comeback requires extinction first — a generation with no living memory of the name, so that when it returns it feels like a discovery instead of a hand-me-down. In 1983 Hazel was, by the numbers, finished. She is now back to 6,432 births a year — 6318 in the latest data — and shows no sign of stopping.

## Why it matters

A flash flood tells you what year it is. A glacier tells you who your grandparents were. A comeback tells you something else: that American parents, given a clean slate, will choose the same names again — minus the memories.

The revival names come from the attic, not the store. Emma, Hazel, Violet, and the rest returned only after the last generation to use them commonly had grown old, so today's parents never met a classroom of them. Extinction, it turns out, is a marketing strategy. Wait long enough, and your great-grandmother's name becomes an original idea.

The average comeback took 33 years to climb from valley to second peak. None of this was planned by anyone. It just keeps happening — generation after generation, the same shortlist of old names cycling back through the nursery.

Every comeback here has a full vital record — living population, median age, year-by-year counts — on its name page. Start with [Emma](/name/Emma/), [Hazel](/name/Hazel/), or [Violet](/name/Violet/), or watch the opposite story in the flash floods that arrived and vanished inside a decade.
