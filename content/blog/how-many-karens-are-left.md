# How Many Karens Are Left?

In 1965, Karen was the third most popular name for girls in America. 32,873 of them were born that year — about 1.8% of all baby girls.

In 2020, the year the meme peaked, 325 names ranked above it. Karen came in at #831.

The internet has a very specific picture of a Karen. She is middle-aged. She has an angular haircut. She is asking for the manager.

The data has a different picture. The typical living Karen is not 45. She is closer to 60. She is more likely to *be* a grandmother than to yell at one.

## The name was already old

Karen was not born in the meme. It was born in the 1950s, peaked in the mid-1960s, and has been falling for sixty years.

| Era | Karen's rank |
| --- | ---: |
| 1965 (peak) | 3 |
| 1970s | 25 |
| 1980s | 84 |
| 2000s | 170 |
| 2019 | 660 |
| 2020 | 831 |

By the time the joke arrived, the name had already left the building. The decline did not start in 2020. The meme just stood next to a name that was three decades into a free fall and took the credit.

That is the difference between a name that is *unfashionable* and a name that is *dated*. Karen is dated. It points at a birth decade the way a rotary phone points at a kitchen wall.

## Counting the living, not the born

Most baby-name sites can tell you how many Karens were *born*. We care about how many are *here*.

Every birth year gets run through an actuarial life table — the same kind insurers use — so we can estimate how many people with a given name are still alive, and how old they are. A name is not a chart. It is a crowd of living people, aging in real time.

Run Karen through that table and two facts fall out.

First, there are still a lot of them — by our estimate, on the order of a million living Americans named Karen. This is not a rare name. It is a common name attached almost entirely to one generation.

Second, and this is the whole story: the median living Karen is about 60 years old. She was born around 1965, at the very top of the wave. She is retiring, not climbing a corporate ladder. Her own kids are grown.

The meme aged the name *down*. Reality is aging it *up*.

## There is no next Karen

The part that makes Karen a Name Vitals story, and not just a meme story, is what is happening at the bottom of the chart.

Almost no one is being named Karen anymore. The youngest Karens are already adults. There is no replacement generation filling in underneath — no nursery full of future Karens to lower the average age back down.

That is the signature of a name sliding from *declining* toward *endangered*: a large living population with a high median age and a birth rate near zero. The crowd is real, but no one new is joining it, and the clock only runs one way.

Compare it with [Elizabeth](/name/Elizabeth/), a name that belongs to every decade at once and so belongs to none of them. Elizabeth has babies, teenagers, mothers, and grandmothers. Its median age sits in the middle of life because the name keeps getting refilled. Karen does not get refilled. Its median age climbs by roughly a year, every year, because the only thing happening to the population of Karens is that it is getting older.

## The joke and the obituary

There is something quietly strange about the most mocked name of the 2020s being, demographically, one of the gentlest. The living Karens are overwhelmingly women in their late 50s and 60s. They named *their* daughters Jessica and Ashley. They did not name anyone Karen, because by the time they were having children the name already sounded like their mother's.

The meme will fade. It already is. But it sped up something that was going to happen anyway. A handful of parents who might once have used the name — for a grandmother, for a tribute, for the sound of it — quietly crossed it off. You can see the extra dip in the years after 2020, a small acceleration at the end of a long decline.

So: how many Karens are left? Around a million, give or take, and the number falls a little every year — not because the name is being replaced, but because it isn't.

The manager Karen demanded to speak to retired in 2024.
