# How America Stopped Sharing Names

Two facts about American names look like they cannot both be true.

The first: we stopped sharing them. In 1880, the ten most popular boys' names covered **44%** of all newborn boys — nearly half a country drawing from one short list. Today the top ten cover **8%**. For girls the slide runs from 25% to 7%. The shared name, the name three kids in every classroom answered to, is gone.

The second fact contradicts the first. Even as we fled the popular names, we *converged* on a smaller set of **sounds** — especially for boys. The single most common ending letter for a boy's name accounted for about 15% of births for the whole first half of the 20th century. Then, starting in the 1960s, it climbed: by 2010 **more than a third of all American boys** got a name ending in the letter N — Aiden, Mason, Logan, Ethan, Jackson, Jaxon. Girls never moved off the vowel: about 37% have ended in a single dominant letter for 145 years, first the **-E** era (Annie, Carrie, Bessie), then the **-A** era (Linda, Lisa, Emma, Olivia).

So: fewer shared *names*, but fewer shared *sounds*. How can both be true at once?

## The reconciliation: we respell

The answer is that American individuality stopped expressing itself through *choice of name* and started expressing itself through **spelling**. Parents take a sound everyone agrees on and write it a way no one else has. A 2010 nursery full of Jaydens, Aidens, Caydens, and Kaidens sounds identical at roll call and looks unique on every name tag. The originality is purely orthographic — and you can watch it detonate, the same explosion caught at three different stages.

**Caitlin** is a complete supernova. Essentially nonexistent before the late 1970s, the sound *KATE-lin* splintered into Caitlin, Caitlyn, Katelyn, Kaitlyn, Katelynn, Katlyn and a dozen more, peaked at **17 spellings** in 1998, and is now nearly extinct in all of them — an entire lifecycle inside one generation.

**-aden** barely existed before 1980. Then a sound that was effectively *invented* shattered into **81 distinct spellings** by 2012 — Jayden, Aiden, Brayden, out to Zaiden, Raiden, Khayden and Caedyn — more than 100,000 boys in a single year at its height. It is now collapsing: once a sound has eighty spellings, none of them feels special, and the whole family caves in.

**-eigh** is the one still running. And it works differently from the other two. Caitlin was one name; -aden was one sound; but **-leigh** is a *productive suffix* — a decorative ending you can bolt onto any root to mint a new daughter's name on demand. Everleigh. Oakleigh. Wrenleigh. Emberleigh. Novaleigh. Riverleigh. It has become a morpheme that means, roughly, *a girl, bespoke.* And the letters do no work at all: Ryleigh is Riley, Charleigh is Charley, Hayleigh is Hailey. ## What the supernova means

Put the three charts together and the paradox dissolves. American individualism did not invent new names, and it did not even invent new sounds. It took a shrinking set of shared sounds and **fragmented them across the page** — Caitlin seventeen ways, Aiden eighty-one, Riley into Ryleigh into anything-leigh without limit. We did not get more creative. We got more creative with the same few letters.

And the supernova is self-consuming. The instant a sound accumulates enough spellings, the distinctiveness each one was bought for evaporates, and parents move to the next frontier. Caitlin's generation has already burned out; -aden's is burning now; -eigh's is still bright. There will be another — there is always another — because the thing being chased was never a name. It was the feeling of being the only one.
